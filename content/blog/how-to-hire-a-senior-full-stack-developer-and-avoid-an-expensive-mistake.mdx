Most hiring mistakes don't come from picking someone who can't code. They come from picking someone who can code but can't own a product. I've been on both sides of this: as a designer handing work to engineers, as a developer on a four-year remote agile team, and as the sole engineer on a production platform. Here's what I'd look for if I were hiring me.

### 1. They've shipped to real users, not just to GitHub

Tutorial projects prove syntax. Production proves judgment. Ask what happened after launch: what broke, how they found out, and what they changed. A senior engineer has stories about 2 a.m. bugs, not just features.

### 2. They can work across the whole stack without handoffs

A true full-stack developer can take a feature from database schema to API to UI. That matters most on small teams, where every handoff costs a day. Ask them to walk you through one feature end to end.

### 3. They care about how it looks and feels

Users judge your product by the interface. I started in UI/UX design in 2019, and it still shapes every screen I build. An engineer who thinks about spacing, loading states and empty states saves you a designer's revision cycle.

### 4. They ask about your business before your tech stack

Good engineers ask who the users are, what success looks like and what the deadline really is. Then they recommend a stack. If the first question is "which framework?", be cautious.

### 5. They communicate without being chased

Remote work runs on written updates. Look for someone who tells you what they did, what's blocked and what's next, before you ask. This is the single biggest predictor of a smooth freelance engagement.

### 6. They can explain trade-offs in plain language

"We can ship this in two weeks with polling, or three with WebSockets, and here's what users will notice." That's the sentence you want to hear. It means they're helping you decide, not just executing.

### 7. They've owned something alone

Being the only engineer on a product forces you to learn deployment, monitoring, security and support. Most recently I was the sole engineer on a live, real-time sports platform. That kind of ownership is hard to fake in an interview.

### A quick hiring checklist

- Ask for one production project they can discuss in depth
- Ask what they'd do in week one on your project
- Start with a small paid milestone before a long contract
- Check how quickly and clearly they reply to messages
